What it is

Most trading demos show you the wins.
This one shows you the whole tape.

An option is a contract giving the right to buy 100 shares at a fixed price before a fixed date. You can only lose what you paid for it — which is why this agent buys them and never sells them.

The hard part of an autonomous trader is not placing an order. It is being able to answer, afterwards, why it did that — and being able to prove the answer. So every minute produces a row: the price, the average it was measured against, the decision, and the reason in plain English.

The AI logic

One fixed loop, once a minute, always in this order

  1. Fetch the minute that just closed. Not arrived within twenty seconds? Skip it and journal the skip. A late bar is missing data, never a reason to guess.
  2. If holding, check the exits first. Target, stop, or held-too-long. If one fires, sell and stop — no new entry is considered this minute.
  3. Only if flat, form a view. Is price more than 0.5% below VWAP — the day’s average weighted by volume, i.e. what the typical dollar paid today — and did this minute trade 20% heavier than usual?
  4. Turn the view into a contract. Nearest strike, expiring tomorrow, sized on the premium at risk — not the ~$77,000 of shares one contract controls.
  5. Refuse if the trade cannot pay for itself. The gap between an option’s buying and selling price is paid twice on a round trip. Over 5% of the contract’s value and the agent declines, because the whole hoped-for gain is 0.4%.

No language model sits in the trading path. The rule is small, fixed and reproducible from the record. An LLM between the market and an order would make every decision unauditable for no measured gain.

The risk gates

The strategy proposes. A separate layer disposes.

Every intended order passes through a risk layer that can only ever say no or smaller. It has no code path that makes anything larger.

That is not a claim in a comment. A property test throws 5,000 randomly built orders at it — random limits, times, position counts, losses — and asserts that not one comes back bigger than it went in. A future change that quietly adds a growth path fails that test without anyone having to anticipate it.

Strategy moduleForbidden by an automated test from importing anything outside Python’s standard library. No network, no clock, no account — so it cannot read the future.
Look-ahead guardReading any bar past the current minute raises an error. A test deliberately reaches into the future and asserts the crash.
The flattenerA separate process on its own schedule closes everything at 15:50 without asking whether the trader is healthy — because the case that matters is the one where it is not.

The Alpaca infrastructure

One file is allowed to touch the network

broker.py drives the Alpaca CLI for everything: the market clock, minute bars, the option chain, live option quotes, account equity, orders, and the flatten-everything calls. Nothing else in the codebase imports subprocess or opens a socket.

Three things we measured rather than assumed

  • The account is proved before every order. alpaca doctor must report the paper endpoint. Anything else, including no answer, stops the order.
  • --profile is silently ignored by doctor. So a run could check one account and trade on another, with the check passing and meaning nothing. The account is fixed once per process by environment variable, and the trader refuses to start unless it is connected to PA3JTED9VTZY.
  • We do not wrap the CLI in our own retry loop. It already retries rate limits three times and honours Retry-After; a second layer multiplies the two and stalls past the minute.

On data: SIP carries all US volume but arrives 15 minutes late on this subscription — fine for backtests, useless live. IEX is real-time but carries ~4% of volume. So we ran the entire study on both feeds to find out whether that gap changes the answer. It does not.

What we measured

We tested the strategy properly, and it does not work

144 combinations of four settings, each against a coin-flip control: identical stops, targets and exits, with entry timing chosen at random. If a rule cannot beat random entry it has no edge — it is just holding the market.

Ask 96 questions and a couple will look good by luck alone; the correction for that is standard, and we applied it. Zero survive, on both feeds, with trading costs set to zero — the most generous test possible. The best contiguous block of settings gives +0.40 basis points per trade over 1,727 trades (t = +0.95, p = 0.343): indistinguishable from luck.

8 / 11

Originality

The fallback was fixed before the result was read

The plan committed in advance: if no setting clears the bar, run the tested rule anyway at quarter size, one position, and publish both numbers. That is what is running. A fallback chosen after seeing the result is not a fallback, it is a rationalisation.

So the live profit and loss this week is a sample of a rule we have already shown we cannot distinguish from chance. Whatever it comes out at, up or down, one week of trading cannot overturn that.

The deliverable here is not a profitable strategy. It is an agent whose every decision — and every refusal — can be checked by someone who does not trust us. That is the part that transfers to a strategy that does work.

Known gaps, stated rather than hidden

What we would not want a judge to have to find

  • No option-level backtest. 2.4 GB of option prices were downloaded and not used. No data tier carries historical bid/ask, so the cost of trading would have to be modelled rather than measured — and modelling it precisely for a rule already shown to have no edge was not the best use of three days.
  • No independent auditor. Our standing rule is that the code checking the rules should be written by someone who never saw the rules. One author, one week: suspended knowingly. The per-minute record still lets someone else check the work. Nobody has.
  • The thresholds are not ours. The entry distance, volume ratio and 15-minute exit came from the competition brief with no stated provenance. We swept all of them rather than trusting them — which is how we found they do not work.

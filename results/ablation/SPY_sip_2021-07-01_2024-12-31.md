# Does any setting of this rule beat a coin flip?

Generated by `scripts/run_ablation.py` over 2021-07-01 to 2024-12-31, SPY share prices,
data fingerprint `221eb3a580a8e6dc`. Trading costs are set to zero, which makes every
number here a ceiling rather than an estimate.

## The short version

We tried **144 combinations** of four settings: how far below the day's
average price to buy, how long to hold, whether to keep trading into the
last twenty minutes, and whether to require heavier-than-usual volume.
Each one was run twice -- once with the rule deciding when to enter, and
once with a coin flip deciding, using the identical stop, target and
exits. The number that matters is the gap between them.

- **96 of 144** combinations can be read at all. **24** are excluded
  because a winning trade in them is worth exactly nothing -- the buy
  price and the target are the same price -- and **24** produced fewer
  than 150 trades, below which the measurement is too fuzzy to mean
  anything in either direction.
- **18 of those 96** beat their own coin flip by any margin at all.
- **0 survive** once we account for having asked 96 questions.

**Nothing survives.** Not one setting of this rule, at zero trading
cost, beats entering at random times with the same exits. The idea is
not merely mistuned.

## How to read the tables

Each cell is **rule minus coin flip, in basis points per trade**. A basis
point is a hundredth of one percent, so +5 means the rule earned 0.05%
more per trade than random entry did. Zero means the rule contributed
nothing and any profit belonged to the market. Cells marked `--` had
fewer than 150 trades; cells marked `no reward` are the ones where a
winning trade is worth zero by construction. Neither is read.

Rows are how far below the day's average price we buy. Columns are how
many minutes we hold before giving up.

Each row also states the share of trades it must win merely to break
even. That bar is not the same down the column: buying deeper means a
bigger gap between the entry and the target, so a winner is worth more
and fewer of them are needed. It is why a raw win rate cannot be
compared between rows, and why the number in the cells is the gap
against that row's own coin flip instead.

## The correction, and why the raw numbers lie without it

Ask 144 questions of pure noise and about seven come back looking
significant at the usual 5% threshold. That is not a flaw in the
threshold, it is what 5% means. The Benjamini-Hochberg correction
handles it by making the bar stricter the further down the ranked list
you read: the best result is judged against 0.0010, the tenth against
0.0104, and so on. We allow at most 10% of whatever we call a discovery
to be a fluke.

## What this means for the decision

1. **This is not a tuning problem.** The rule was not merely set
   wrong; no setting of it works. Widening the search further would
   only raise the number of questions asked, which makes the
   correction stricter, not the rule better.

2. **The ceiling failed, so everything below it fails.** These runs
   charge nothing to trade. Real trading costs money, and the trade
   we actually intend -- buying an option contract -- costs far more
   per round trip than a share does. A rule that cannot make money
   for free cannot make money at a price.

3. **The sealed holdout stays sealed.** There is no candidate to test
   on it. Opening it now would spend the one measurement we have left
   on a rule we already know does not work.

4. **What this does not say.** It says nothing about whether SPY
   reverts to its daily average over hours or days -- only that it
   does not do so within four hours in a way this entry rule can
   catch. And it says nothing about the option layer's own
   behaviour, which is a separate question we have not asked.
